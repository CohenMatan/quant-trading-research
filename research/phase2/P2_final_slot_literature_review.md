# Final Phase 2 slot: literature review (P2-CP10, 2026-10-02)

- **Purpose:** assess the external evidence that the remaining, genuinely distinct strategy families can let a **long-only, large-cap, concentrated** US equity portfolio **beat S&P 500 buy-and-hold after costs** (owner items 5–8).
- **What was not used:**
  - no 2010–2021 return of any factor or candidate measure was computed;
  - no measure was compared with another on our data;
  - no post-2021 performance was consulted.
- **Citations** are given from memory with year and journal. **Every number must be verified against the original before it is quoted elsewhere.** Where I am unsure of a magnitude I state the direction only.
- **Labels used throughout:**
  - **[pre-2010]:** evidence published (or circulated) before our development period;
  - **[later]:** published after 2010, often covering our development window;
  - **[practice]:** practitioner conventions;
  - **[ours]:** our inference.

## 1. Value (book-to-market and close relatives)

### 1.1 The original evidence [pre-2010]

| Source | Finding | Bearing on our objective |
|---|---|---|
| Basu (1977), JF | Low price/earnings stocks earned higher risk-adjusted returns (NYSE, 1957–1971) | The earliest systematic value evidence (an earnings-yield form) |
| Rosenberg, Reid & Lanstein (1985), JPM | High book/price stocks outperformed | First book-to-market evidence |
| Fama & French (1992), JF | Size and book-to-market (B/M) together capture the cross-section of average returns, 1963–1990; beta adds nothing | The canonical result. **Financial firms are excluded,** because high leverage means something different for them |
| Fama & French (1993), JFE | The HML factor (high minus low B/M) | The value factor used ever since |
| Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny (1994), JF | Value beats glamour, mostly through investors extrapolating past growth too far; it is not simply riskier | **Behavioural rationale** that does not require small stocks |
| Fama & French (1995), JF; Chen & Zhang (1998) | High B/M firms are persistently less profitable and more often distressed | **Risk rationale:** value is partly compensation for distress, which implies value traps |
| Loughran (1997), JFQA | Among the **largest** stocks, B/M had little explanatory power outside January (1963–1995); the premium sits mostly in small stocks | **The most important negative for us:** our universe is ≥ $2B |
| Fama & French (1998), JF | Value premium in 12 of 13 major markets | Not a US-only artefact |
| Davis, Fama & French (2000), JF | US value premium back to 1929 | Long-sample robustness |
| Asness, Porter & Stevens (2000, working paper); Cohen & Polk (1998, working paper) | Much of B/M's power is within-industry; industry-relative B/M performs at least as well | Sector concentration matters, and a raw-B/M book is partly an industry bet |
| Piotroski (2000), JAR | Most of the high-B/M premium comes from a minority of winners; many high-B/M firms keep deteriorating | **Value traps** are the norm, not the exception, in a concentrated value book |
| Schwert (2003), Handbook of the Economics of Finance | Several anomalies weakened or vanished after publication; value was among the more persistent | Some persistence, but decay must be expected |
| Lev & Sougiannis (1996), JAE | Capitalising R&D changes measured book values materially | **Book equity omits internally created intangibles,** a structural comparability problem that grows with the knowledge economy |
| Chan & Lakonishok (2004), FAJ | Review: value worked across size groups and markets, with long, painful spells of underperformance (notably 1998–2000) | Long underperformance periods are part of the strategy |

### 1.2 Large caps, long-only, costs [pre-2010 data, some published later]

- **Size dependence.**
  - Fama & French (2006, JFE; 2012, JFE): the value premium is larger among small stocks than big stocks, though usually positive among big ones.
  - Israel & Moskowitz (2013, JFE; data back to 1926): the value premium weakens with firm size, and is weak among the largest stocks. Unlike momentum, a meaningful part of value's return comes from the long side. *Magnitudes to verify.*
- **Costs.**
  - Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016, RFS): value is a **low-turnover** strategy, and most of its gross premium survives realistic trading costs.
  - Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2015/2018, working paper; real institutional trades): costs of value portfolios are small.
- **Timeliness.** Asness & Frazzini (2013, JPM): measuring B/M with the **current** market value (rather than six-month-old prices) improves value's performance. It also makes value more negatively correlated with momentum.
- **Diversification.** Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013, JF), "Value and momentum everywhere": value works across markets and asset classes, and is negatively correlated with momentum.

### 1.3 Post-publication and recent evidence [later]

**These papers cover our 2010–2021 development window. They describe, in public, roughly what a value strategy experienced in our development data. This is a contamination risk, disclosed in §1.6.**

- **McLean & Pontiff (2016), JF.** Average anomaly returns are about 26% lower out of sample and about 58% lower after publication.
- **Fama & French (2021), Review of Asset Pricing Studies, "The value premium."** The US value premium was lower in 1991–2019 than in 1963–1991, and the later estimate is statistically weak.
- **Israel, Laursen & Richardson (2021), JPM, "Is (systematic) value investing dead?"** Value's 2018–2020 drawdown came mainly from value spreads widening (cheap stocks getting cheaper), not from cheap firms' fundamentals deteriorating.
- **Arnott, Harvey, Kalesnik & Linnainmaa (2021), FAJ, "Reports of value's death may be greatly exaggerated."** The weakness is attributed partly to intangibles and partly to valuation-spread changes.
- **Lev & Srivastava (2022), Critical Finance Review; Eisfeldt, Kim & Papanikolaou (2022), Critical Finance Review.** Book-based value is distorted when intangible capital is not on the balance sheet. Intangible-adjusted measures do better.

### 1.4 Practitioner conventions [practice]

- **Construction.**
  - Style indices (e.g. Russell 1000 Value, S&P 500 Value) use **composites** of several ratios (book, earnings, sales), weight by market cap, and hold hundreds of names.
  - Academic factors use B/M, exclude financials, and rebalance annually.
- **Long-only value funds and ETFs** typically hold 50–400 names with sector constraints. Concentrated 20-name deep-value books are rare and known for high tracking error and value-trap risk.
- **Benchmarks.** Value managers are usually judged against a value index, not against the S&P 500. Beating the S&P 500 is a harder target than "beating value peers".

### 1.5 Interaction with profitability (background only)

- Novy-Marx (2013, JFE) and Fama & French (2015, JFE): profitability and value are negatively correlated, and each improves the other in a combination.
- Asness, Frazzini & Pedersen ("Quality minus junk"): cheap and high-quality together.

**This is background only. It does not authorise a multi-factor H017** (owner item 4), and H016's profitability measure stays rejected.

### 1.6 What this means for us [ours]

1. **Credible but modest.**
   - **For:** the oldest, most replicated cross-sectional premium; a clear economic rationale; very low turnover; and point-in-time data we have already verified (equity matches SEC filings within 0.5% in 96.4% of reports, X971).
   - **Against:**
     - the evidence is weakest exactly where we must operate (large caps, long-only, concentrated);
     - it decays after publication;
     - book equity is structurally degraded as an accounting measure.
2. **Plausible size of the edge.**
   - My reading of the pre-2010 large-cap evidence: a long-only top-B/M book in large caps beat its universe by perhaps **0–3% a year** in the original samples, with higher volatility and deep, multi-year underperformance spells.
   - After publication decay, a forward-looking expectation is towards the lower end of that range.
3. **Beating SPY is harder than beating the universe.**
   - The cap-weighted index is the target, while our controls are equal-weight.
   - The universe's own gap to SPY (equal weight versus cap weight) adds to or subtracts from any factor edge. In H016's universe, equal weight trailed SPY by about 0.5 point a year in 2010–2021.
4. **Contamination, disclosed and not used to steer.**
   - It is public knowledge (§1.3) that US value, especially large-cap value, did poorly over most of 2010–2020.
   - **This knowledge points against value in our development window.** It is not a reason to pick value, which would be hindsight in the favourable direction.
   - It does mean that a value failure in development would teach us little we did not already suspect. A value pass would have succeeded against a known headwind.
   - I have not used this to rank the families. I report it because the owner must weigh it before spending the last slot.

## 2. Net share issuance / net payout yield [pre-2010 core evidence]

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Ikenberry, Lakonishok & Vermaelen (1995), JFE | Open-market buyback announcers outperform over the following years, concentrated in value stocks |
| Loughran & Ritter (1995), JF | Equity issuers underperform for years after issuing |
| Daniel & Titman (2006), JF | Composite issuance (market-value growth not explained by returns) predicts returns |
| Pontiff & Woodgate (2008), JF | Share issuance predicts returns post-1970, independent of B/M, size and momentum |
| Fama & French (2008), JF, "Dissecting anomalies" | Net issuance is among the few anomalies robust **in all size groups, including big stocks** |
| Boudoukh, Michaely, Richardson & Roberts (2007), JF | Net payout yield (dividends + net repurchases) predicts returns better than dividend yield |
| Stambaugh, Yu & Yuan (2012), JFE [later] | Anomaly profits, issuance among them, come mostly from the **short** leg (over-priced issuers) |

**What it means for us [ours]:**
- **Credible, distinct and large-cap-robust.** But a long-only book captures mainly the weaker "buyback" leg.
- **Data path not yet validated.**
  - Share counts are unsafe as levels: they are split-adjusted to today (D107).
  - A market-value-based construction (growth in market cap minus price return) uses only point-in-time fields. But it has **never been audited**: market cap's share-update timing, dual-class firms, and stock-for-stock mergers.
  - It would need an infrastructure audit (no slot) before it could be a hypothesis. **Not ready for the final slot today.**

## 3. Investment / asset growth

- **Evidence:**
  - Cooper, Gulen & Schill (2008), JF: firms with high total-asset growth underperform.
  - Titman, Wei & Xie (2004), JFQA.
  - Fama & French (2015), JFE: the investment factor CMA.
- **Against us:**
  - Fama & French (2008) report the asset-growth effect is **weak among big stocks**.
  - CMA is highly correlated with HML: in a long-only book it is largely a value tilt by another name.
- **What it means for us [ours]:** data-feasible (total assets is approved), but weak where we operate, and largely redundant with value. Not recommended.

## 4. Accruals / earnings quality

- **Evidence:**
  - Sloan (1996), Accounting Review: firms with high accruals underperform.
  - Green, Hand & Soliman (2011), Management Science, "Going, going, gone?": the anomaly largely disappeared in the 2000s (data before our window).
- **What it means for us [ours]:** decayed before our period, and close to the profitability family the owner excluded. Not recommended.
